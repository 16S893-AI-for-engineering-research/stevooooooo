Teaching AI the physical robot

Oct 27, 2026

Work Done

  • Built a complete URDF of the cell (two SO-101s with the custom gripper and camera mounts, the DexArm with the laser camera, the base mount and the tube) to replace raw CAD for the agents.
  • Agent turned the URDF into the robot model: mesh collision checking (python-fcl) wired into the motion safety layer, a pose viewer, and a DexArm collision check using the DexArm firmware’s own kinematics.
  • Diagnosed the non-moving wrist and gripper servos: servos 4–6 on both arms are encoder-only leader-arm servos. Replacement and recalibration tracked as task AA-43.

Notes

Big lesson this week was that making AI understand something physical in real life is not easy. Especially when a model is as complicated as this is with 3 different robotic arms with many many parts, it has a hard time distinguishing things. I did not make it easier since alot of the parts in my model are named the same thing. Burned quite a bit of money on that mistake. Instead of just giving it the CAD, I ended up creating a urdf myself to give the agents. This made things much more understandable for it. I also realized I need more of one kind of the servo before I can complete everything and will need to rebuild the arms so that's fun. Went on a goose chase trying to figure that out, as well. Thought I had fried something but turned out they were dummy servos with just encoders so you could use them as a leader arm.

Recreating a paper’s simulation from scratch

Sep 22, 2026

Work Done

  • Rebuilt the simulation from Jonckers et al. (2022), “Additive Manufacturing of Large Structures Using Free-Flying Satellites”; it reproduces 16 of 16 published quantities within tolerance (full write-up on the Simulation page).
  • Clarify-before-build questions changed the build: no full arm inverse kinematics (the paper treats the arm as an ideal follower), and nozzle friction calibrated against the reported 8.42 mm result and labelled as calibrated, since the paper gives no friction value.
  • Kept the model 3 DoF: ELISSA is a planar air-bearing table and the paper is explicitly 3 DoF.
  • Found that the arm must point along the body y axis to reproduce the reported error split (0.68 mm x vs 0.28 mm y), and that Equation 3 appears transposed; documented the working version rather than silently fixing it.
  • Hypothesis property tests found a wrong test (zero torque is correct when travel is parallel to the lever arm) and a real bug (the print was centred on the free-flyer’s centre of rotation, cancelling the 400 mm lever arm).
  • With no PyPI access in the sandbox, the agent wrote a small shim to run the real tests unmodified, and emitted figures as hand-written SVG (5–33 KB each).

Notes

This project was super interesting. I don't believe the AI actually ever finished its thinking. It ran out of space twice. After that, I checked what it had written up to that point and it seemed complete but I think there is a good chance it would have continued working. I ulitmately decided not to try and let it finish because this project broke the $20 dollar mark in terms of tokens already. I think such stringent requirements in my prompt forced the AI to do alot more iterations than might have been totally necessary. Besides the AI, I noticed some of my own bias creeping in as a manager. This session most definitely ended in a cost overrun and technically the session wasn't even finished yet. This is partially because I found myself in a sunk cost fallacy. Since I couldn't actually know how much more progress there was to complete, I kept assuming that it was only a little bit more until eventaully I had spent $20.
